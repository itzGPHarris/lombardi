## Most Valuable Parent wants to be the air traffic controller for your family's week

Every parent with school-age kids knows the feeling of standing in the kitchen at 7:25 am, trying to remember whether today is the day that needs sneakers. The information exists somewhere. It is in a newsletter that arrived on Sunday, a photo of the rotation calendar on the fridge, a WhatsApp thread with forty unread messages and a text from the violin teacher. The problem is that it is everywhere at once, and one person in the house is expected to hold all of it.

Most Valuable Parent, a new planning app from [company], is built for that person. Plenty of new apps promise an AI assistant, and MVP has one too, but that isn't the interesting part. What MVP gets right is the plan itself: one view of the family's day that looks less like a productivity app and more like the departures board at a train station, that is honest about who has agreed to what, and that stays connected to the people who actually share the work. It is one of the more thoughtful takes on family logistics we have seen.

### It meets you where the mess already is

The smartest decision MVP makes is not asking you to copy anything. The app shows up in your phone's share menu, so you can press and hold a message in WhatsApp, Messages or Mail and send it straight to MVP. Each account gets a private forwarding address, and you can point a school's weekly newsletter at it and never think about it again. You can photograph a handout, or tap the microphone and ramble for twenty seconds while you are stuck in the pickup line.

MVP also connects to the calendar you already keep, whether that is Google Calendar, Apple Calendar or Outlook, and it brings in what is already there: the therapy appointment every Monday, dance on Wednesdays, ballet on Thursdays. The connection only reads. MVP never adds or changes anything on your calendar, which is a rarer and more reassuring choice than it sounds. The payoff comes later in the week. When the sample parent booked her daughter's checkup through the pediatrician's own portal, the appointment landed on her Google Calendar, and MVP noticed within moments that it fell in the middle of a Friday tutoring session. It offered two ways to fix it and drafted the text to the tutor. Nobody had to tell MVP the appointment existed.

What comes back is a short read-back that tells you what MVP took and what it ignored. With the sample family MVP uses to show the app, it read a full elementary school newsletter and kept exactly two things for a fourth grader: an early dismissal on Thursday and a free dress day on Friday. It told the parent, in plain words, that the fifth-grade field trip and the rest of the newsletter were not for her daughter. Anyone who has scrolled a newsletter looking for their kid's grade will feel that one.

MVP also understands that a school calendar is not just a list of events. Photograph a kindergarten rotation calendar, where each letter day means library, science or motor skills, and MVP turns it into standing reminders, so "Library book" and "Sneakers" appear on the right mornings without anyone typing them in. Every item it creates keeps a link back to the document it came from, which is the right call for a product asking parents to trust it with dates.

### Today is a departures board, not a calendar

MVP's home screen, called Today, shows only today. The next departure sits at the top with a countdown, such as Alexis leaving with the carpool in 15 minutes, and under it is a short checklist of what goes with her. Below that, each person in the house gets a row through the day, so you can see at a glance that the afternoon depends on you being at one school at 2:30 and another at 2:45. In the evening the screen turns to what needs to be ready tonight and how tomorrow starts, which is when most of the real work of getting kids out the door happens.

The rest of the week lives in a separate Game plan tab, which opens with a list of everything that still needs attention: things the coach has suggested, people you have asked who have not answered, and anything that collides with something else. You can drag an item to another day or put someone's name against it without talking to the coach at all.

### It never pretends the plan is done

The most distinctive thing about MVP is its honesty about uncertainty. Every item has an owner, which might be "You", your partner's name, or "Nobody yet", and a status such as "Asked Edward" or "Edward: yes". The app never calls a day sorted or covered. When a plan changes, as it does for the sample family when a dad who had agreed to two Thursday pickups gets pulled into a meeting, the pickup he can no longer make reopens on its own and MVP lays out the options.

That is the other thing worth noticing. When something needs a decision, MVP offers two or three ways it could go, recommends one and explains why, and it treats choosing a different option as completely normal. The sample parent passes on the recommended fix because she doesn't like the alternative yoga class, which is exactly the kind of thing software could never know. The app assumes the parent is in charge, and it is built that way throughout.

### It connects you to your people, and you stay in charge

Most family apps treat the other adults in your life as an afterthought. MVP puts them at the center. Your inner circle, meaning your partner, your family and the people you trust with your kids, and your village, meaning the school parents and carpool families who share your week, are part of the plan rather than names in a contact list. When part of the plan belongs to someone else, MVP drafts the message in your voice, in the first person, and you edit and send it yourself. If the other person has MVP, the request shows up in their app and they tap "I've got it" or "I can't do this". If they don't, they get a text with a link to a single page with the same two buttons, and no account is needed. For a parents' WhatsApp group or a school office, MVP hands the text to the app you choose and adds a reminder to your list. A line under every draft says it plainly: you send this, and MVP never sends anything for you.

The privacy model is equally clear. People see only what you send them. Your partner sees the requests you send him, not your whole plan or your conversations with the coach. You can create private groups for the other parents in your life, such as your kids' class parents, and MVP's assistant never reads them. That is where the questions no app can answer go, such as which therapist at the practice down the road is taking new patients. In a market full of apps that want access to your calendar and inbox, MVP asks for one thing at a time.

### A helper for the questions, too

MVP's assistant, which the app calls the coach, also answers the questions parents usually take to Reddit, and it answers from what it knows about your family. It is one helper among several rather than the star of the show. When the sample parent asked how to get her six-year-old through picture day morning without a meltdown, the coach suggested settling the outfit the night before by offering two choices, and it listed the details it based that on, such as her daughter's age and the dress she had already noted. A short article from MVP's own staff went deeper. The coach is careful about its limits. It does not recommend therapists or sitters, and when the parent learned her daughter's therapist was leaving, it pointed her to her school-parent group rather than guessing.

### What we'd like to see

A few things will decide whether MVP works in daily life. The calendar connection only reads, so MVP's own plan, such as who is collecting whom, never shows up on your calendar, and power users will ask for that. There is no email connection beyond forwarding, which is a deliberate trust decision. It is English only for now. And its reading of messy school documents has to be very good, because the whole product rests on parents believing the dates it gives them.

### The verdict

MVP is not trying to be another shared calendar. It is trying to take the sorting, filtering and chasing off the one parent who does all of it, while leaving every decision and every message in her hands. That restraint is what makes it interesting. The AI is table stakes, and the plan, the honesty and the people are the product. If the capture holds up across a real school year, MVP could earn a permanent spot on a lot of home screens.
